# Personal publications: editorial craft

One writer can offer a quick verdict on a link, spend an essay defending a
decision, or take a whole tutorial to change a reader's mind. Listen for
what each piece promises in its opening and where it earns the ending.

## Labnotes Weekend Reading

[A sourdough finisher](https://labnotes.org/weekend-reading-a-sourdough-finisher/),
[A server raclette](https://labnotes.org/weekend-reading-a-server-raclette/), and
[More time to write](https://labnotes.org/weekend-reading-more-time-to-write/).

The recurring title prefix identifies the series. The changing subtitle can
be whimsical, topical, or a pun, and need not summarize every item. The
newsletter format does much of the work: recognizable sections such as “Tech
Stuff” collect independent links, quotations, images, and short annotations.
Within that structure, Assaf Arkin's personal selection and commentary
provide the voice. An annotation can name the use, audience, concrete
feature, personal reaction, or limitation. It does not need all five. In “A
sourdough finisher,” he qualifies a claimed startup-speed advantage by
explaining that the underlying code still runs on Node. “A sourdough
finisher” opens directly with the tech section. “A server raclette” and “More
time to write” first offer a separate entertainment link. “A server raclette”
moves from technical tools through design, people, business, AI, and security
to a miscellaneous section whose last entries include a library story and a
friendly sign-off. The items can range from a single sentence to a quoted
passage with context, and the issue ends with subscription material rather
than a forced synthesis.

Arkin gives a reader enough information to pass on a link as well as to
follow it. Try that on a developer tool. “The viewer remembers the filter
you used last time. You still need a server to run it.” A reader with a
static site can move on, and both sentences have earned their keep.

## David Heinemeier Hansson

[Seven years from China](https://world.hey.com/dhh/seven-years-from-china-a63b03cc),
[The malleable computer](https://world.hey.com/dhh/the-malleable-computer-7c187a9b), and
[Setting the pace](https://world.hey.com/dhh/setting-the-pace-03d4539a).

These titles name a time and place, a central idea, or a practical action.
Each makes a different promise. “Seven years from China” proceeds from
earlier trips to recent observations, then moves through factory and
transport examples to a geopolitical argument. It acknowledges the limited
view of a short visit and the author's unusual reception, but still draws
ambitious conclusions. It ends by returning to the personal experience of the
visit. “The malleable computer” is much shorter: it states a promise,
identifies a barrier, proposes a change, gives an operating-system
distinction and an example, then closes with a broader prediction. “Setting
the pace” begins with a management principle and grounds it in the six-week
practice of the author's company.

The six-week cycle gives Hansson somewhere to stand. He can be emphatic
about how his company works because a reader can see the practice that
produced his opinion. In the China piece, the reader can also see how much
of the country he saw on that visit, and judge the leap he makes from it.

Give an editorial about release trains the same footing. Suppose the
author's account says the fix was ready on Tuesday and releases went out
on Fridays. “We shipped it on Friday because Friday was on the calendar.”
The second Friday is where you hear the frustration. The rest of the piece
can explain who set that calendar and whether its original purpose still
holds.

## Marco Arment

[Ten years after we lost Steve Jobs](https://marco.org/2021/10/05/steve-jobs-ten-years),
[The future of the App Store](https://marco.org/2021/09/13/future-of-the-app-store), and
[Ten years of Overcast: A new foundation](https://marco.org/2024/07/16/overcast-rewrite).

The sampled titles range from a personal anniversary to a broad forecast to
an anniversary with a specific product-change subtitle. Their forms differ as
much as their subjects. The Jobs remembrance is short, unsectioned, and
openly personal: it says the writer never met Jobs, then explains what the
loss meant to him and ends with a sense of unrealized work. The App Store
essay opens with a prediction, enumerates expected rules, and walks through
consequences for users and developers, including hypothetical behavior by
named companies. It ends by restating the preferred course. Its predictions
and interpretations are arguments from 2021, not present-day facts. The
Overcast announcement leads with the release, then organizes new features,
retained features, removals, next steps, and the reason for the rewrite. It
explains the user-visible consequence of removing streaming and puts
technical detail in a footnote before a note of thanks.

Arment makes room in a release announcement for something the reader
has lost. Follow the person who used the old feature. If an app now has
to download an episode before playing it, what happens when a listener
presses play on the platform and the train goes into a tunnel? The first
wait may tell a returning user more than the list of improvements.

In the remembrance the premise is different. Arment establishes how Jobs
mattered to him even though they never met. Once that relationship is
clear, the author can write about a loss at its actual, personal scale.

## Wait But Why

Tim Urban's New Year piece works almost entirely by changing the reader's
reference point. From January 2020, West Germany's 1974 World Cup win was
closer to the first tournament than to the present. A few lines later the
comparison involves the reader's own childhood. The arithmetic stays simple
as the stakes get personal. Repeating the construction is part of the pleasure:
the reader learns the game and still gets caught out.
[It's 2020 and you're in the future](https://waitbutwhy.com/2020/01/its-2020-and-youre-in-the-future.html)

The procrastination essay takes longer to get there. A dictionary definition
is little comfort to someone who has put off a thesis. An embarrassing story
gives the reader a reason to follow a cast of imaginary characters, whose
behavior changes when a deadline looms. The long AI essay opens with a
question about where we stand on a curve. It builds up the vocabulary needed
to ask a much harder question by the end of Part 1. Its predictions belong
to 2015. What travels well is the pacing: introduce a term when the reader
has a question it can answer.
[Why Procrastinators Procrastinate](https://waitbutwhy.com/2013/10/why-procrastinators-procrastinate.html) ·
[The AI Revolution, Part 1](https://waitbutwhy.com/2015/01/artificial-intelligence-revolution-1.html)

For an engineering explainer, try showing one quantity from two seats.
Suppose 120 people submit a job at noon. A single worker handles one every
30 seconds. The first person gets a result before 12:01. The last gets theirs
at one o'clock. Now the reader has a reason to ask what the dashboard means
by “30 seconds per job.”

## Overreacted

[Goodbye, Clean Code](https://overreacted.io/goodbye-clean-code/),
[The Elements of UI Engineering](https://overreacted.io/the-elements-of-ui-engineering/),
and
[A Complete Guide to useEffect](https://overreacted.io/a-complete-guide-to-useeffect/).

The titles include a farewell to an idea, a map of a field, and a
comprehensive technical guide. In “Goodbye, Clean Code,” a late-night
refactoring scene and two code organizations let the reader understand why
the change initially seemed attractive. The next morning supplies the
reversal. The essay then identifies both a collaboration failure and a
technical tradeoff before ending with advice to reconsider the goal of
cleanliness. “The Elements of UI Engineering” instead introduces the author's
attempt to articulate tacit knowledge, catalogs distinct UI problems and
tradeoffs, and ends by inviting readers to experiment on a small interface.
The `useEffect` guide is much longer: it returns to examples, common mistaken
mental models, and edge cases as it builds an explanation. Its closing points
back to the opening summary and invites feedback. Its 2019 framework advice
and forecasts are historical. Check current documentation before applying
them.

The recurring craft is to make the reader's reasoning visible: demonstrate a
plausible approach, test it against a concrete change or edge case, and
revise the model. Questions, personal mistakes, code, and exercises serve
that explanation in different combinations. They are not required features of
every technical article, and a reconstructed or simplified code example
should be labeled accordingly.

Choose an example small enough to follow, demonstrate the tempting solution, then
introduce a specific requirement that exposes its tradeoff. Explain what changed in the
reasoning and offer a next step only if it helps the reader practice. For example, show a
cache keyed by user ID, then add a locale-dependent response and explain why the key must
account for locale. Verify the behavior in the relevant system. Don't invent a failure
story to make the lesson dramatic.
