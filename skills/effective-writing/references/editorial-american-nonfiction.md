# American nonfiction for technology writers

These readings help with different drafts. A pile of interviews might need
John McPhee's way of finding a structure. A polished page that could have
been written by anybody might need William Zinsser's ear for the person
doing the explaining. The engineering examples below are fictional.

## When the teacher has a voice

In *On Writing Well*, William Zinsser addresses nonfiction writers as a
teacher. His essay about finding that voice contains a surprise. As a young
columnist he cultivated the urbane ease he admired in E. B. White. Years
later, helping individual students with their drafts gave him the voice he
wanted for his book. For a bylined engineering piece, look for the moment
when the author drops the presentation voice to help someone see what they
saw. An aside from a code review can be a better starting point than the
carefully worded project abstract.
[Zinsser on finding the voice for his book](https://theamericanscholar.org/looking-for-a-model/)

Suppose an engineer's notes say, “I kept checking the database. The typo
was on the invoice.” Once those words are on the page, the reader has a
question about the gap between the record and the document. Follow one
invoice through the renderer. Save the inventory of other output formats
for an article in which one of those formats makes a difference.

## When the material is better than the outline

John McPhee opens “Structure,” collected in *Draft No. 4*, lying on a picnic
table after months of reporting on the Pine Barrens. He already has enough
material. His difficulty is finding a shape for it. Elsewhere in the essay
he describes arranging encounters around a recurring figure and choosing an
ending before writing his way toward it. Try his experiment on a migration
diary. Circle the moment the team made the call to stay. Lay out only the
things a reader needs in order to understand why staying was once the less
likely choice. A week of installing tools might take one line. The ten
minutes when a rehearsal went wrong might take half the piece.
[McPhee's essay “Structure”](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2013/01/14/structure)

Jack Hart's *Storycraft* offers another choice. In his account of editing
reported stories, a single scene can carry a vignette, and an explanatory
narrative can teach through the unfolding event. Ask what actually changed
during the reporting. If the author revised a belief, give the earlier
belief a fair hearing and show the evidence that undid it. If the software
simply did something curious, it can hold the stage by itself.
[Hart on choosing a narrative form](https://niemanstoryboard.org/2011/10/20/jack-hart-storycraft-narrative-nonfiction-interview/)

Consider a restore rehearsal. The backup contains every byte of a photo.
After the restore the album looks empty because its index lives elsewhere.
For a short explainer, stay with one photo from upload to empty album to
rebuilt index. For a team decision, start with the old sign-off criterion.
Why had a successful byte count seemed sufficient? Which test would the
team now insist on? An ending you could earn with that reporting: “This
time we signed off after we could open the album.” It gives the change in
policy a last visible action.

## When the most interesting word is buried

Roy Peter Clark's *Writing Tools* treats word order as an editorial
decision. A period lends weight to what comes just before it. Try ending
the same observation twice. “We still had the photos when we opened the
empty album” ends on the user's experience. “When we opened the empty
album, we still had the photos” ends on the clue that could solve it.
Choose according to what the next paragraph does. Clark also recommends
returning to a key word or image across a story. The word *album* can mean
a blank screen at the beginning and a working restoration test at the end.
[Clark's writing tools at Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/archive/2002/if-i-were-a-carpenter-the-tools-of-the-writer/)

Verlyn Klinkenborg's *Several Short Sentences About Writing* gives the
writer a prior task: pay attention to what catches your own attention.
During revision, recover one detail the notes preserved and the draft
smoothed away. “Recovery succeeded” might have replaced the author's more
interesting observation that the thumbnails came back a minute before
the pictures did. If that minute explains how the system works, let the
reader spend a paragraph there. There is no prize for the shortest sentence.
[Publisher's excerpt from Klinkenborg](https://penguinrandomhousesecondaryeducation.com/book/?isbn=9780307958495)

## When the reporting is already funny

Science writer Mary Roach describes thinking about humor while selecting
material for a book. A situation can be inherently odd, and her position
as an outsider can supply the joke. For a tool review, keep an eye on what
made the author laugh during the test. In an imagined setup guide the
installer prints a farewell banner, then spends another four minutes
installing. If the author timed it, “Its goodbye lasted four minutes” is
enough. The next paragraph can explain what the installer still had to do.
A table of timings alone supports a perfectly good straight account.
[Roach on finding humor in her reporting](https://www.livescience.com/54987-grunt-military-science-mary-roach-interview.html)
