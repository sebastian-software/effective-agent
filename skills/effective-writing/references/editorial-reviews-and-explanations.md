# Reviews, judgments, and explanations

Read the section closest to the question your reader brings. These are close
readings of individual pieces. The links identify the originals for a reader
who wants to study them. The observations and examples here work offline.

## Wirecutter

A reader choosing between two tools needs a verdict early enough to use and a
reason to trust the comparison. In three Wirecutter articles published in
partnership with Engadget, the category guides advertise a clear pick in their
titles. The collection of learning toys promises a selection. The podless
coffee maker headline takes a third approach: it names the two contenders and
asks which is better. Only the headline of that last piece was available for
this comparison. [Podless coffee maker comparison](https://www.nytimes.com/wirecutter/reviews/keurig-lavazza-podless-coffee-makers/)

The grinder guide moves from lab extraction tests to how a grinder feels in a
kitchen. The glove guide changes its recommendation with the weather and how
precisely the wearer needs to use a phone. The toy guide looks at what children
can do with an object in a school lab or at home, then organizes the selection
by activity. Notice how much explanation the qualification can save. A reader
can recognize the circumstance that changes the answer and go straight to it.
These historical partner editions illustrate the editorial method.
[Coffee grinders](https://www.engadget.com/best-coffee-grinder-170000648.html) ·
[Touchscreen winter gloves](https://www.engadget.com/2016-02-19-the-best-touchscreen-winter-gloves.html) ·
[Learning and STEM toys](https://www.engadget.com/2016-12-02-learning-toys-and-stem-toys-we-love.html)

For an engineering comparison, decide what would make a competent reader choose
the runner-up. A dependency checker might be easy to adopt in a single package
and painfully slow in a workspace. Put the reader who maintains thirty packages
in the article. Show the work required in that setting if you tried it. An
untested case can remain an open question. End when the reader can make the
choice, even if different readers will choose differently.

## Daring Fireball

John Gruber gives a long review just the product name. A first-impressions
title gives an early verdict a time limit. A short follow-up can use a playful
title because its opening scene will supply the subject. In the iPhone piece,
a checkerboard appears during a drag when the page can't redraw fast enough.
That tiny imperfection tells the reader which part of the experience took
priority. In the Vision Pro review, windows stay in the kitchen when their
owner walks away. In the follow-up, a virtual butterfly seems to tickle some
people's fingers. Gruber tells us he didn't feel it. The distinction between
his own experience and someone else's makes a strange report easier to trust.
[iPhone First Impressions](https://daringfireball.net/2007/06/iphone_first_impressions) ·
[The Vision Pro](https://daringfireball.net/2024/01/the_vision_pro) ·
[Simple Tricks and Nonsense](https://daringfireball.net/2024/02/simple_tricks_and_nonsense)

For a bylined review, find a moment that reveals the designer's priorities.
What did the program keep doing when it could no longer do everything? Show
the visible behavior before interpreting it. The reader now has enough to
disagree with the verdict. A product-name headline works when the audience
already has a question about that product. Give unfamiliar work a more
informative title.

## The Verge

In Nilay Patel's two reviews the title pairs a product name with an editorial
verdict or a play on the feature under test. The expression earns its place
when readers can find the incident behind it. For Vision Pro, Patel arranges
windows around himself and considers what someone in the same room can share.
For iPhone X, the distance between his face and the phone becomes painfully
concrete outdoors as he tries to unlock it. Both episodes put a big product
promise in contact with a mundane physical constraint.
[Apple Vision Pro review](https://www.theverge.com/24054862/apple-vision-pro-review-vr-ar-headset-features-price) ·
[iPhone X review](https://www.theverge.com/2017/10/31/16579748/apple-iphone-x-review)

Patel's Decoder introduction about Google gives a theory a short name, then
follows its consequences into one small publisher's business. The title's
question leads into the interview. This is one editor working in two genres.
For a review, put the advertised convenience through a task the reader
recognizes. For an interview-led piece, give the question to someone who lives
with its consequences and let their evidence complicate the premise.
[Google Zero is here — now what?](https://www.theverge.com/24167865/google-zero-search-crash-housefresh-ai-overviews-traffic-data-audience)

## TechCrunch

Brian Heater enters his Vision Pro review through Scott McCloud's idea of a
comic extending beyond the page. The cultural detour gives readers a picture
of a workspace extending beyond the screen. His first encounter with the
Rabbit R1 uses a launch-event detail to convey the mood, then puts a time limit
on what he can judge from a few hours of use. Devin Coldewey's later R1 column
asks what an admittedly unfinished device might be worth to a particular
buyer. His imagined walk belongs to a possible future. Those two R1 titles
announce different degrees of familiarity with the very same object.
[Heater on Vision Pro](https://techcrunch.com/2024/02/03/apple-vision-pro-review-the-infinite-desktop/) ·
[Heater's first R1 impressions](https://techcrunch.com/2024/04/24/rabbits-r1-is-a-little-ai-gadget-that-grows-on-you/) ·
[Coldewey's R1 column](https://techcrunch.com/2024/05/05/the-rabbit-r1-shipped-half-baked-but-thats-kind-of-the-point/)

A useful detour supplies a concept that keeps paying off after the introduction.
If a compiler author compares a dependency graph to a transit map, the map
should help the reader reason about a transfer, closure, or alternative route
later in the article. If the interesting material is a first hour with a tool,
name that duration and make room for the surprise that survived it.

## Julia Evans

The titles give readers a particular puzzle, an admission of difficulty, or
a question about teaching. In the pipe article a second filter appears to stop
a command from printing. Readers can hold almost everything constant while
they watch the effect of changing one thing. In the colors article, the topic
opens out: the author's own difficulty leads to other users' problems and
several layers of software that can affect the result. The essay on explaining
technical concepts names an audience with real knowledge and identifies the
specific missing idea. You can assume a reader knows how to use a shell while
showing them something about output buffering they have never needed to know.
[Why pipes sometimes get “stuck”](https://jvns.ca/blog/2024/11/29/why-pipes-get-stuck-buffering/) ·
[Terminal colours are tricky](https://jvns.ca/blog/2024/10/01/terminal-colours/) ·
[How do you write simple explanations without sounding condescending?](https://jvns.ca/blog/2020/11/15/simple-explanations-without-sounding-condescending/)

For a mechanism readers can reproduce, let them make a prediction before
showing the output. Explain the discrepancy while the question is fresh.
For a problem with multiple owners, follow the value across boundaries and
show where each component can change it. That second piece may deserve a much
longer middle than the first.
