# Product Operating Model

Use this route when the product problem is not only what to build, but how a
team should own outcomes, explore options, make commitments, and learn. Treat
the operating model as a set of behaviors and decision rights, not a ritual,
org-chart template, or collection of fashionable role names.

## Start with a durable team and an outcome

A product team is a persistent cross-functional owner of customer and business
progress. Give it:

- a target customer or product area
- an outcome or problem to improve
- relevant strategic context and constraints
- access to customers, product behavior, operations, and economics
- authority to explore solutions within clear boundaries
- accountability for quality, business viability, and learning after release

An empowered team is not free from constraints. It understands why the outcome
matters and can choose how to pursue it. A feature team receives solutions,
optimizes delivery, and is then blamed for outcomes it was not allowed to
influence.

Rewrite feature assignments as:

```text
target situation + desired outcome + evidence + constraints + decision window
```

Keep true commitments explicit. Do not relabel a fixed executive solution as an
“outcome” to simulate empowerment.

## Share discovery across disciplines

Product, design, and engineering bring different primary responsibilities:

- **Product management** keeps customer value, business viability, strategy,
  evidence quality, prioritization, and stakeholder alignment connected.
- **Product design** develops the problem model, interaction system, usability,
  experience quality, and direct understanding of people in context.
- **Engineering leadership** exposes technical possibilities and constraints,
  protects system quality and operability, and finds enabling technology or
  simpler implementation approaches.

All three participate in discovery, evaluate risks, and see important evidence
firsthand. The product manager is neither a backlog secretary nor the lone
inventor or “CEO of the product.” The designer is not a downstream decorator.
Engineers are not merely estimators for decisions made elsewhere.

Assign a clear decision owner where needed, but make the reasoning and dissent
visible. Strong product decisions usually emerge from the tension between
desirability, usability, feasibility, business viability, trust, and
distribution—not from one function winning by default.

## Connect discovery and delivery

Continuous discovery does not mean endless interviews, and continuous delivery
does not mean shipping every generated idea. Maintain two connected flows:

- discovery reduces uncertainty through evidence, options, prototypes, and
  commitment tests
- delivery turns sufficiently supported decisions into reliable product
  behavior and exposes the next evidence

Keep engineers and designers close enough to current discovery that feasibility
and experience insights arrive before roadmap commitments. Keep product
managers close enough to delivery that operational consequences, scope
pressure, and quality tradeoffs change the decision when necessary.

## Explore broadly before converging

Cheap generation changes the expected breadth of exploration. For a
consequential but reversible opportunity, challenge the team to examine
**10–20 materially different approaches per week** before settling on the first
plausible answer, and carry at least two genuinely distinct candidates into
decision-grade testing when the risk and timeline permit. Run the exploration
with the filters and limits in
[Discovery and evidence](discovery-and-evidence.md#exploit-cheap-exploration-without-faking-evidence):
twenty generated screens are not twenty product ideas, and a polished AI
simulation is not market evidence.

## Make high-integrity commitments

Separate a forecast, a target, and a commitment:

- a **forecast** is the team's current evidence-based expectation
- a **target** is a desired outcome that still contains uncertainty
- a **commitment** is a promise to another party with understood consequences

Before a consequential commitment:

1. Name the customer and business result.
2. Review value, usability, feasibility, viability, trust, and distribution
   risks.
3. State what has been learned directly and what remains assumed.
4. Define scope and the critical quality bar.
5. Include product, design, and engineering in the commitment.
6. Record dependencies, confidence, decision date, and change conditions.

Discovery before commitment is not a guarantee. It is how the team avoids
promising work whose basic value or feasibility it has not examined.

## Adapt the model to the environment

The constraints shift by stage:

- An early company may need the same people to discover, sell, support, and
  deliver while keeping demand claims especially disciplined.
- A growing product may need clearer team missions, interfaces, platform
  boundaries, and portfolio choices before dependencies consume autonomy.
- An established organization may need to unwind funding, procurement,
  governance, and executive habits that reward project output over product
  outcomes.

Do not infer a required team size, title set, release cadence, or research
volume from the stage label. Diagnose the actual bottleneck.

## Change the surrounding system

Empowered teams cannot compensate indefinitely for:

- annual project funding tied to fixed solution lists
- leaders who override discovery while delegating outcome accountability
- inaccessible customers or analytics
- incentives based only on utilization, velocity, or feature count
- approval chains that make small reversible tests as expensive as launches
- architecture that prevents bounded team ownership

Recommend the smallest operating change that creates a useful feedback loop:
one outcome-owned team, one product area, direct evidence access, explicit
guardrails, a short decision window, and leadership review of learning and
outcomes rather than output volume.

Hack days, prototypes, and idea challenges can expose possibilities and give
engineers room to innovate. They complement, but do not replace, persistent
ownership, customer access, and decision rights.

## Operating-Model Deliverable

Return:

1. current decision and delivery pattern
2. outcome, customer, scope boundary, and constraints
3. role responsibilities and shared discovery work
4. exploration portfolio and filtering cadence
5. evidence and commitment gates
6. surrounding organizational blockers
7. smallest operating change, owner, review window, and success signals
